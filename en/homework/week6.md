> **Basic Knowledge: Even and Odd Permutations, the Alternating Group**<br>A permutation is called odd (even) if it can be written as a product of an odd (even) number of transpositions. The set $A_n$ of all even permutations of $n$ elements is a normal subgroup of $S_n$, called the alternating group on $n$ elements, and for $n\ge2$ we have $\left|A_n\right|=\frac{n!}{2}$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Conjugation of Cycles**<br>For any $\sigma\in S_n$ and any $r$-cycle $\left(i_1i_2\cdots i_r\right)$, we have $\sigma\left(i_1i_2\cdots i_r\right)\sigma^{-1}=\left(\sigma\left(i_1\right)\sigma\left(i_2\right)\cdots\sigma\left(i_r\right)\right)$. Hence two cycles in $S_n$ are conjugate if and only if they have the same length; in particular, all transpositions are conjugate to one another.

> **Basic Knowledge: The Sign Homomorphism**<br>The determinant homomorphism in Example 1.4.17 yields $\mathrm{sgn}:S_n\to\left\{1,-1\right\}$, $\sigma\mapsto\mathrm{sgn}\left(\sigma\right)$, where the sign of a transposition is $-1$; if $\sigma$ is written as a product of $m$ transpositions, then $\mathrm{sgn}\left(\sigma\right)=\left(-1\right)^{m}$; in particular, the sign of a product of $n$ disjoint transpositions is $\left(-1\right)^{n}$, and $\ker\mathrm{sgn}=A_n$.

> **Basic Knowledge: The Alternating Group Is Generated by 3-Cycles**<br>For $n\ge3$, $A_n$ is generated by all 3-cycles: since $\left(ij\right)\left(ik\right)=\left(jik\right)$ and $\left(ij\right)\left(kl\right)=\left(ijk\right)\left(jkl\right)$, the product of two transpositions is a product of 3-cycles, and every element of $A_n$ can be written as a product of an even number of transpositions.

> **Basic Knowledge: A Normal Subgroup Is a Union of Conjugacy Classes**<br>If $N\triangleleft G$, then $N$ is a union of some conjugacy classes of $G$; in particular, if $x\in N$, then the conjugacy class of $x$ is contained in $N$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Centralizers of Transpositions and Recognizing Transpositions**<br>In $S_n$, the centralizer of a transposition $\left(ij\right)$ has size $2\left(n-2\right)!$; for $n\ne6$, the transpositions form the unique conjugacy class of involutions of this size, so any automorphism maps transpositions to transpositions. For $n=6$, $S_6$ has outer automorphisms, mapping transpositions to involutions such as the square of a 6-cycle.

> **Basic Knowledge: The Center and Inner Automorphisms of a Group**<br>Let $G$ be a group. The map $\mathrm{Ad}:G\to\mathrm{Inn}\left(G\right)$, $a\mapsto\mathrm{Ad}_a$, where $\mathrm{Ad}_a\left(g\right)=aga^{-1}$, is a surjective homomorphism with $\ker\mathrm{Ad}=C\left(G\right)$ (the center of $G$); hence by the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms $\mathrm{Inn}\left(G\right)\cong G/C\left(G\right)$. For $n\ge3$, the center of $S_n$ is $C\left(S_n\right)=\left\{e\right\}$, so $\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_n\right)\cong S_n$.

> **Basic Knowledge: A Finite Group of Even Order Has an Element of Order Two**<br>If a finite group $G$ has even order, then $G$ contains an element of order two: pair the elements of $G$ by mutually inverse pairs; $e$ forms its own pair, and the non-involutions pair off, so since $\left|G\right|$ is even there must be an odd number of involutions, hence a non-identity element of order two exists.

> **Basic Knowledge: The Conjugacy Classes of $S_4$**<br>The conjugacy classes of $S_4$ and their sizes are: $\left\{e\right\}$ (1), transpositions (6), products of two transpositions of type $\left(12\right)\left(34\right)$ (3), 3-cycles (8), and 4-cycles (6). Moreover $K_4=\left\{e,\left(12\right)\left(34\right),\left(13\right)\left(24\right),\left(14\right)\left(23\right)\right\}$ and $A_4$ are normal subgroups of $S_4$, and $K_4$ is isomorphic to the Klein four-group.

> **Basic Knowledge: The Outer Automorphisms of $S_6$**<br>$\mathrm{Out}\left(S_6\right)=\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_6\right)/\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_6\right)\cong\mathbb Z_2$, hence $\left|\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_6\right)\right|=2\left|\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_6\right)\right|=2\cdot6!=1440$.

> **Theorem 1.2.22 (Lagrange's Theorem)**<br>Let $G$ be a finite group and $H<G$. Then $\left|G\right|=\left[G:H\right]\cdot\left|H\right|$.

> **Theorem 1.4.10 (Cayley's Theorem)**<br>Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_G$; in fact the map $L:G\to L_G$, $a\mapsto L_a$ is a monomorphism, where $L_a\left(g\right)=ag$ is the left translation.

> **Lemma 1.4.13**<br>Let $f:G\to G'$ be a group homomorphism. Then $\mathrm{Im}\,f<G'$ and $\ker f\triangleleft G$.

> **Example 1.4.17**<br>Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space over a field $P$. Then the map $\det\circ\pi:S_n\to P^{*}$, $\sigma\mapsto\det\left(\pi_\sigma\right)$ is a group homomorphism whose image is $\left\{1,-1\right\}$ (for $n>1$); its kernel is denoted $A_n$.

> **Theorem 1.4.19 (Fundamental Theorem of Group Homomorphisms)**<br>Let $f:G\to G'$ be a surjective group homomorphism. Then $G/\ker f\cong G'$.

> **Definition 1.6.1 (Cycle)**<br>Let $\left\{i_1,i_2,\cdots,i_r\right\}\subseteq\left\{1,2,\cdots,n\right\}$. If $\sigma\in S_n$ satisfies $\sigma\left(i_1\right)=i_2$, $\sigma\left(i_2\right)=i_3$, ..., $\sigma\left(i_r\right)=i_1$ and fixes all other letters, then $\sigma$ is called an $r$-cycle, denoted $\sigma=\left(i_1i_2\cdots i_r\right)$. In particular, a $2$-cycle is called a transposition.

> **Theorem 1.6.4 (Cycle Decomposition)**<br>Every element $\sigma$ of $S_n$ can be written as a product of some disjoint cycles of $S_n$; disregarding order, this representation is unique.

> **Lemma 1.6.7 (Decomposition of a Cycle into Transpositions)**<br>Every $r$-cycle can be written as a product of $r-1$ transpositions: $\left(i_1i_2\cdots i_r\right)=\left(i_1i_r\right)\left(i_1i_{r-1}\right)\cdots\left(i_1i_3\right)\left(i_1i_2\right)$.

> **Proposition 1.6.8**<br>Every permutation of $n$ elements can be written as a product of transpositions.

> **Theorem 1.6.9 (Simplicity of the Alternating Group)**<br>For $n\ge5$, $A_n$ is a simple group.

## 1.6-5

Prove that

$$
S_n=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(12\cdots n\right)\right\rangle,\quad A_n=\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\cdots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle.
$$

### Solution 1.6-5

**Part 1: prove that $S_n=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle$.** By **Proposition 1.6.8**, $S_n$ is generated by all transpositions. For any transposition $\left(ij\right)$ with $i\ne1$, $j\ne1$, by **Basic Knowledge: Conjugation of Cycles**, taking $\sigma=\left(1i\right)$ gives $\left(1i\right)\left(1j\right)\left(1i\right)=\left(\sigma\left(1\right)\sigma\left(j\right)\right)=\left(i,j\right)=\left(ij\right)$. Hence every transposition $\left(ij\right)$ can be expressed in terms of $\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)$, so $S_n\subseteq\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle$; the reverse inclusion is clear, so $S_n=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle$.

**Part 2: prove that $S_n=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(12\cdots n\right)\right\rangle$.** Write $\sigma=\left(12\cdots n\right)$. By **Basic Knowledge: Conjugation of Cycles**, for $k=1,2,\cdots,n-1$,

$$
\sigma^{k-1}\left(12\right)\sigma^{-\left(k-1\right)}=\left(\sigma^{k-1}\left(1\right)\sigma^{k-1}\left(2\right)\right)=\left(k,k+1\right),
$$

so the adjacent transpositions $\left(12\right),\left(23\right),\cdots,\left(n-1,n\right)$ all belong to $\left\langle\left(12\right),\sigma\right\rangle$. Since the adjacent transpositions generate $S_n$ (by **Proposition 1.6.8**, any transposition $\left(ij\right)$ can be written as a product of adjacent transpositions), we have $S_n\subseteq\left\langle\left(12\right),\sigma\right\rangle$; the reverse inclusion is clear, so $S_n=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(12\cdots n\right)\right\rangle$.

**Part 3: prove that $A_n=\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\cdots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$.** By **Basic Knowledge: The Alternating Group Is Generated by 3-Cycles**, $A_n$ is generated by all 3-cycles. We show that every 3-cycle belongs to $\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\cdots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$.

(1) A 3-cycle containing $1$ and $2$ is $\left(12k\right)$ or its inverse, which is in the generating set.

(2) A 3-cycle $\left(1jk\right)$ containing $1$ but not $2$ ($j,k\ne2$): a direct computation gives $\left(12k\right)\left(12j\right)^{-1}=\left(12k\right)\left(1j2\right)=\left(1jk\right)$, so $\left(1jk\right)$ belongs to the generated group.

(3) A 3-cycle $\left(ijk\right)$ not containing $1$: by (2), both $\left(1ij\right)$ and $\left(1ik\right)$ belong to the generated group, and hence $\left(1ij\right)^{-1}\left(1ik\right)=\left(1ji\right)\left(1ik\right)=\left(ikj\right)$. Since $\left(ikj\right)=\left(ijk\right)^{-1}$, we have $\left(ijk\right)$ in the generated group.

Hence all 3-cycles belong to $\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\cdots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$, so $A_n\subseteq\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\cdots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$. Conversely, each generator $\left(12k\right)$ is a 3-cycle, hence an even permutation (**Basic Knowledge: Even and Odd Permutations, the Alternating Group**), so it belongs to $A_n$; the reverse inclusion holds. Therefore $A_n=\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\cdots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$. $\blacksquare$

## 1.6-6

Prove that the nontrivial normal subgroups of $S_n$ are $A_n$ or $K_4$ (when $n=4$).

### Solution 1.6-6

Let $N\triangleleft S_n$, $N\ne\left\{e\right\}$, $N\ne S_n$.

**Case $n\ge5$.** By **Theorem 1.6.9 (Simplicity of the Alternating Group)**, $A_n$ is simple. Since $N\triangleleft S_n$ and $A_n\triangleleft S_n$ (by **Basic Knowledge: Even and Odd Permutations, the Alternating Group**), the intersection $N\cap A_n\triangleleft A_n$, so by the simplicity of $A_n$ we have $N\cap A_n=\left\{e\right\}$ or $A_n$.

If $N\cap A_n=A_n$, then $A_n\subseteq N$. Since $\left|A_n\right|=\frac{n!}{2}$, the index of $A_n$ in $S_n$ is $2$ (**Basic Knowledge: Even and Odd Permutations, the Alternating Group**), so any subgroup containing $A_n$ is $A_n$ or $S_n$; since $N\ne S_n$, we have $N=A_n$.

If $N\cap A_n=\left\{e\right\}$, then for any $x,y\in N\setminus\left\{e\right\}$, since $x,y$ are both odd permutations, $xy$ is an even permutation, so $xy\in N\cap A_n=\left\{e\right\}$, hence $y=x^{-1}$, i.e. $\left|N\right|\le2$, and the non-identity element of $N$ has order two. By **Basic Knowledge: A Normal Subgroup Is a Union of Conjugacy Classes**, $N$ is a union of conjugacy classes; since the center of $S_n$ is trivial for $n\ge3$ (**Basic Knowledge: The Center and Inner Automorphisms of a Group**), the conjugacy class of the non-identity element $x\ne e$ has size greater than $1$, contradicting $\left|N\right|\le2$. Hence $N\cap A_n=\left\{e\right\}$ is impossible.

Therefore, for $n\ge5$, the only nontrivial normal subgroup of $S_n$ is $A_n$.

**Case $n=4$.** $\left|S_4\right|=24$ and $\left|A_4\right|=12$, with $A_4\triangleleft S_4$. By **Basic Knowledge: The Conjugacy Classes of $S_4$**, the conjugacy class sizes of $S_4$ are $1,6,3,8,6$; by **Basic Knowledge: A Normal Subgroup Is a Union of Conjugacy Classes** and **Theorem 1.2.22 (Lagrange's Theorem)**, the order of a normal subgroup must be a sum of some conjugacy class sizes and must divide $24$, so the possible orders are $1,4,12,24$, corresponding to the normal subgroups $\left\{e\right\}$, $K_4$, $A_4$, $S_4$. Hence the nontrivial normal subgroups are $A_4$ and $K_4$.

**Case $n=3$.** $\left|S_3\right|=6$ and $\left|A_3\right|=3$, with $A_3\triangleleft S_3$ of index $2$. By **Basic Knowledge: A Normal Subgroup Is a Union of Conjugacy Classes**, the conjugacy class sizes of $S_3$ are $1,3,2$, so the possible orders of a normal subgroup are $1,3,6$; hence the normal subgroups are $\left\{e\right\},A_3,S_3$, and the nontrivial one is $A_3$.

**Case $n\le2$.** $S_n$ has no nontrivial normal subgroups.

In summary, the nontrivial normal subgroups of $S_n$ are $A_n$ (for all $n\ge3$) and $K_4$ when $n=4$. $\blacksquare$

## 1.6-11

Let $\left|G\right|=2n$, where $n$ is odd. Prove that $G$ has a normal subgroup of index $2$.

### Solution 1.6-11

**Step 1: construct the group homomorphism $\psi:G\to\left\{1,-1\right\}$.** By **Theorem 1.4.10 (Cayley's Theorem)**, the left regular representation $\lambda:G\to S_G$, $g\mapsto L_g$ (where $L_g\left(x\right)=gx$) is a monomorphism; regard $S_G$ as $S_{2n}$ (since $\left|G\right|=2n$). By **Example 1.4.17** and **Basic Knowledge: The Sign Homomorphism**, the sign homomorphism $\mathrm{sgn}:S_{2n}\to\left\{1,-1\right\}$ is a group homomorphism. The composition $\psi=\mathrm{sgn}\circ\lambda:G\to\left\{1,-1\right\}$ is a group homomorphism.

**Step 2: prove that $\psi$ is surjective.** By **Basic Knowledge: A Finite Group of Even Order Has an Element of Order Two**, $G$ contains an element $a$ of order two (since $\left|G\right|=2n$ is even). Then $\lambda\left(a\right)=L_a$, and for any $x\in G$, $L_a^2\left(x\right)=a^2x=x$, so $L_a$ is an involution; moreover, since $a\ne e$, if $L_a\left(x\right)=x$, i.e. $ax=x$, then $a=e$, a contradiction, so $L_a$ has no fixed points. Hence $L_a$ is a product of $n$ disjoint transpositions on the $2n$ letters of $G$, and by **Basic Knowledge: The Sign Homomorphism**, $\mathrm{sgn}\left(L_a\right)=\left(-1\right)^{n}=-1$ (since $n$ is odd). Therefore $\psi\left(a\right)=-1$, while $\psi\left(e\right)=1$, so $\psi:G\to\left\{1,-1\right\}$ is a surjective homomorphism.

**Step 3: take the kernel to obtain a normal subgroup of index $2$.** Let $H=\ker\psi$. By **Lemma 1.4.13**, $H\triangleleft G$. By **Theorem 1.4.19 (Fundamental Theorem of Group Homomorphisms)**, $G/H\cong\left\{1,-1\right\}$, so $\left|G/H\right|=2$, i.e. $\left[G:H\right]=2$. Hence $G$ has a normal subgroup $H$ of index $2$. $\blacksquare$

## 1.6-14

Prove that $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_n\right)\cong S_n$ when $n\ne2,6$. Also find $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_2\right)$ and $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_6\right)$.

### Solution 1.6-14

**First prove that $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_n\right)\cong S_n$ for $n\ne2,6$.** By **Basic Knowledge: The Center and Inner Automorphisms of a Group**, $\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_n\right)\cong S_n/C\left(S_n\right)$, and for $n\ge3$ we have $C\left(S_n\right)=\left\{e\right\}$, so $\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_n\right)\cong S_n$. It therefore suffices to prove $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_n\right)=\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_n\right)$, i.e. every automorphism is inner.

Let $\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_n\right)$. An automorphism preserves the order of elements, so involutions are mapped to involutions. By **Basic Knowledge: Centralizers of Transpositions and Recognizing Transpositions**, for $n\ne6$ the transpositions form the unique conjugacy class of involutions of this type (with centralizer size $2\left(n-2\right)!$), and an automorphism preserves conjugacy classes and centralizer sizes, so $\varphi$ maps transpositions to transpositions.

Let $\varphi\left(12\right)=\left(ab\right)$. Compose $\varphi$ with the inner automorphism $\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma}$, where $\sigma\in S_n$ satisfies $\sigma\left(a\right)=1$, $\sigma\left(b\right)=2$; then $\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma}\varphi\left(12\right)=\left(\sigma\left(a\right)\sigma\left(b\right)\right)=\left(12\right)$. Hence we may assume $\varphi\left(12\right)=\left(12\right)$.

For $k=3,\cdots,n$, since $\left(12\right)\left(1k\right)=\left(1k2\right)$ is a 3-cycle of order $3$, the transposition $\varphi\left(1k\right)$ must have product with $\varphi\left(12\right)=\left(12\right)$ being a 3-cycle; hence $\varphi\left(1k\right)$ must share exactly one letter with $\left(12\right)$, i.e. $\varphi\left(1k\right)=\left(1,j_k\right)$ or $\left(2,j_k\right)$ ($j_k\ne1,2$). If for some $k$ we have $\varphi\left(1k\right)=\left(2,j_k\right)$, composing with the inner automorphism $\mathrm{Ad}_{\left(12\right)}$ (which fixes $\left(12\right)$ and swaps the letters $1,2$) lets us uniformly write each $\varphi\left(1k\right)$ in the form $\left(1,\sigma\left(k\right)\right)$, where $\sigma\in S_{n-1}$ is a permutation of $\left\{3,\cdots,n\right\}$ (fixing $1,2$). Hence $\varphi$ acts on the generators $\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)$ exactly as the inner automorphism $\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma}$ does.

By **Exercise 1.6-5, Part 1**, $\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\cdots,\left(1n\right)$ generate $S_n$, so $\varphi$ and $\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma}$ agree on the generators and hence on all of $S_n$; thus $\varphi=\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_n\right)$.

The above argument fails for $n=6$, because $S_6$ has outer automorphisms (by **Basic Knowledge: Centralizers of Transpositions and Recognizing Transpositions** and **Basic Knowledge: The Outer Automorphisms of $S_6$**). Hence, for $n\ne2,6$,

$$
\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_n\right)=\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_n\right)\cong S_n.
$$

**Find $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_2\right)$.** $S_2=\left\{e,\left(12\right)\right\}$ is a cyclic group of order two, and an automorphism must fix the identity $e$, so $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_2\right)=\left\{\mathrm{id}\right\}$ is the trivial group.

**Find $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_6\right)$.** By **Basic Knowledge: The Outer Automorphisms of $S_6$**, $\mathrm{Out}\left(S_6\right)\cong\mathbb Z_2$ and $\left|\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_6\right)\right|=\left|S_6\right|=720$ (since $C\left(S_6\right)=\left\{e\right\}$), so $\left|\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_6\right)\right|=\left|\mathrm{Out}\left(S_6\right)\right|\cdot\left|\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_6\right)\right|=2\cdot720=1440$. $\blacksquare$
