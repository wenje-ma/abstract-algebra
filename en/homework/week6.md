> **Definition 1.6.1** (Cycle) Let
> $$
> \left\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{r}\right\}\subseteq\left\{1,2,\dots,n\right\}.
> $$
> If $\sigma\in S_{n}$ satisfies $\sigma\left(i_{1}\right)=i_{2}$, $\sigma\left(i_{2}\right)=i_{3}$, ..., $\sigma\left(i_{r}\right)=i_{1}$ and fixes the remaining letters, then $\sigma$ is called an $r$-cycle, denoted $\sigma=\left(i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{r}\right)$. In particular, a $2$-cycle is called a transposition.

> **Theorem 1.6.4** (Cycle Decomposition) Every element of $S_{n}$ can be expressed as a product of pairwise disjoint cycles in $S_{n}$. Up to the order of the factors, the expression is unique.

> **Lemma 1.6.7** (Decomposition of a Cycle into Transpositions) Any $r$-cycle can be written as a product of $r-1$ transpositions:
> $$
> \left(i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{r}\right)=\left(i_{1}i_{r}\right)\left(i_{1}i_{r-1}\right)\dots\left(i_{1}i_{3}\right)\left(i_{1}i_{2}\right).
> $$

> **Basic Knowledge** (Alternating Group) When a permutation can be expressed as a product of an odd (even) number of transpositions, it is called an odd permutation (even permutation). The set $A_{n}$ of all even permutations on $n$ letters is a normal subgroup of $S_{n}$, called the alternating group on $n$ letters, and $\left|A_{n}\right|=\left|S_{n}\right|/2=n!/2$.

> **Theorem 1.6.9** (Simplicity of the Alternating Group) When $n\ge 5$, $A_{n}$ is a simple group.

> **Theorem 1.2.22** (Lagrange's Theorem) Let $G$ be a finite group and $H<G$. Then $\left|G\right|=\left[G:H\right]\cdot\left|H\right|$.

### Problem 1

(Problem 1.6-5) Prove:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{n}&=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\dots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(12\dots n\right)\right\rangle,\\
A_{n}&=\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\dots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Solution to Problem 1

**First part:** $S_{n}=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\dots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle$.

$S_{n}$ is generated by all transpositions. Take any transposition $\left(ij\right)$ with $i\ne 1$, $j\ne 1$. By the conjugation formula,

$$
\begin{aligned}\left(1i\right)\left(1j\right)\left(1i\right)&=\left(\left(1i\right)\left(1\right),\left(1i\right)\left(j\right)\right)\\&=\left(i,j\right)\\&=\left(ij\right).\end{aligned}
$$

Hence any transposition $\left(ij\right)$ can be expressed in terms of $\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\dots,\left(1n\right)$, and therefore

$$
S_{n}=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\dots,\left(1n\right)\right\rangle.
$$

**Second part:** $S_{n}=\left\langle\left(12\right),\left(12\dots n\right)\right\rangle$.

Write $\sigma=\left(12\dots n\right)$. By the conjugation formula, for $k=1,2,\dots,n-1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}\sigma^{k-1}\left(12\right)\sigma^{-\left(k-1\right)}&=\left(\sigma^{k-1}\left(1\right)\sigma^{k-1}\left(2\right)\right)\\&=\left(k,k+1\right).\end{aligned}
$$

Hence the adjacent transpositions $\left(12\right),\left(23\right),\dots,\left(n-1,n\right)$ all lie in $\left\langle\left(12\right),\sigma\right\rangle$. Since adjacent transpositions generate $S_{n}$, $\left\langle\left(12\right),\sigma\right\rangle=S_{n}$.

**Third part:** $A_{n}=\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\dots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$.

By **Lemma 1.6.7** (Decomposition of a Cycle into Transpositions) and **Theorem 1.6.4** (Cycle Decomposition), $A_{n}$ is generated by all $3$-cycles (a product of two transpositions, if disjoint, is itself a product of two $2$-cycles and can be expressed as a product of $3$-cycles; a general even permutation decomposes into an even number of transpositions, and hence into a product of products of pairs of transpositions, i.e. a product of $3$-cycles).

We now show that any $3$-cycle can be expressed in terms of $\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\dots,\left(12n\right)$.

(1) A $3$-cycle containing both $1$ and $2$ is either $\left(12k\right)$ or its inverse, already in the generating set.

(2) A $3$-cycle containing $1$ but not $2$, i.e. $\left(1jk\right)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}\left(12k\right)\left(12j\right)^{-1}&=\left(12k\right)\left(1j2\right)\\&=\left(1jk\right).\end{aligned}
$$

(3) A $3$-cycle not containing $1$, i.e. $\left(ijk\right)$: by (2) we first obtain $\left(1ij\right)$ and $\left(1ik\right)$, and then

$$
\begin{aligned}\left(1ij\right)^{-1}\left(1ik\right)&=\left(1ji\right)\left(1ik\right)\\&=\left(ikj\right)\\&=\left(ijk\right).\end{aligned}
$$

Hence all $3$-cycles lie in $\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\dots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle$. Conversely, each generator is an even permutation, so it is contained in $A_{n}$. Therefore

$$
A_{n}=\left\langle\left(123\right),\left(124\right),\dots,\left(12n\right)\right\rangle.
$$

$\blacksquare$

### Problem 2

(Problem 1.6-6) Prove that the nontrivial normal subgroups of $S_{n}$ are $A_{n}$, or $K_{4}$ when $n=4$.

### Solution to Problem 2

Let $N\triangleleft S_{n}$, $N\ne\left\{e\right\}$, $N\ne S_{n}$.

**Case $n\ge 5$:** By **Theorem 1.6.9** (Simplicity of the Alternating Group), $A_{n}$ is a simple group.

Consider $N\cap A_{n}$. Since $N\triangleleft S_{n}$ and $A_{n}\triangleleft S_{n}$, we have $N\cap A_{n}\triangleleft A_{n}$. By the simplicity of $A_{n}$, either $N\cap A_{n}=\left\{e\right\}$ or $N\cap A_{n}=A_{n}$.

If $N\cap A_{n}=A_{n}$, then $A_{n}\subseteq N$. Since $N\ne S_{n}$, $N$ contains no odd permutations. Also $N\cap A_{n}=A_{n}\subsetneq N$, while $S_{n}/A_{n}$ has only two elements, so $N=A_{n}$.

If $N\cap A_{n}=\left\{e\right\}$, then $N$ contains no even permutations (other than the identity), i.e. every nonidentity element of $N$ is odd. In this case, the product of any two nonidentity elements of $N$ is even, and hence must be $e$; i.e. every nonidentity element of $N$ is of order $2$ and pairwise commutes. Also $N\triangleleft S_{n}$, so $N$ consists of conjugates of transpositions (or products of disjoint transpositions). But all transpositions in $S_{n}$ are conjugate, so if $N$ contains one transposition it must contain all transpositions, and hence $N=S_{n}$, a contradiction. Therefore this case is impossible.

Thus for $n\ge 5$, the only nontrivial normal subgroup of $S_{n}$ is $A_{n}$.

**Case $n=4$:** $S_{4}$ has order $24$. $A_{4}$ has order $12$ and is normal. Moreover,

$$
K_{4}=\left\{e,\left(12\right)\left(34\right),\left(13\right)\left(24\right),\left(14\right)\left(23\right)\right\}
$$

is a normal subgroup of $A_{4}$ and is invariant under conjugation, so $K_{4}\triangleleft S_{4}$.

The normal subgroups of $S_{4}$ are only $\left\{e\right\},K_{4},A_{4},S_{4}$. Hence the nontrivial normal subgroups are $A_{4}$ and $K_{4}$.

**Case $n=3$:** The normal subgroups of $S_{3}$ are only $\left\{e\right\},A_{3},S_{3}$; the nontrivial one is $A_{3}$.

**Case $n\le 2$:** Trivial, or no nontrivial normal subgroup.

In summary, the nontrivial normal subgroups of $S_{n}$ are $A_{n}$ (for all $n\ge 3$), and $K_{4}$ when $n=4$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 3

(Problem 1.6-11) Let $\left|G\right|=2n$ where $n$ is odd. Prove that $G$ has a normal subgroup of index $2$.

### Solution to Problem 3

Consider the left regular representation of $G$: by Cayley's theorem, the map

$$
\lambda:G\to S_{G}, g\mapsto L_{g},
$$

where $L_{g}\left(x\right)=gx$, is a monomorphism. Regard $\lambda$ as a monomorphism from $G$ to $S_{2n}$.

Take the sign homomorphism $\mathrm{sgn}:S_{2n}\to\left\{1,-1\right\}$. The composite is

$$
\mathrm{sgn}\circ\lambda:G\to\left\{1,-1\right\}.
$$

This is a group homomorphism. We now prove it is surjective.

Since $\left|G\right|=2n$ is even, $G$ contains an element $a$ of order $2$ (pair up the elements of $G$ as mutual inverses; $e$ pairs with itself, and the remaining non-order-$2$ elements pair up in pairs; since the order is even, there must be an odd number of order-$2$ elements, so a nonidentity order-$2$ element exists).

$\lambda\left(a\right)=L_{a}$ is a permutation of $G$. Since $a^{2}=e$, for any $x\in G$, $L_{a}^{2}\left(x\right)=a^{2}x=x$, so $L_{a}$ is an involution. Also $a\ne e$, and $L_{a}$ has no fixed points (if $ax=x$ then $a=e$). Therefore $L_{a}$ is a product of $n$ disjoint transpositions, and its sign is

$$
\begin{aligned}\mathrm{sgn}\left(L_{a}\right)&=\left(-1\right)^{n}\\&=-1,\end{aligned}
$$

where we used that $n$ is odd. Hence $\mathrm{sgn}\circ\lambda$ is a surjective homomorphism.

Let $H=\ker\left(\mathrm{sgn}\circ\lambda\right)$. By the fundamental homomorphism theorem, $G/H\cong\left\{1,-1\right\}$, so $\left[G:H\right]=2$.

Also $H$ is a kernel, so $H\triangleleft G$. That is, $G$ has a normal subgroup of index $2$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 4

(Problem 1.6-14) Prove that when $n\ne 2,6$, $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{n}\right)=S_{n}$. Also find $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{2}\right)$ and $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{6}\right)$.

### Solution to Problem 4

We first prove that $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{n}\right)=S_{n}$ when $n\ne 2,6$.

Clearly the inner automorphism group of $S_{n}$ satisfies $\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_{n}\right)\cong S_{n}/Z\left(S_{n}\right)$. When $n\ge 3$, the center of $S_{n}$ is trivial, so $\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_{n}\right)\cong S_{n}$. It suffices to show that every automorphism is inner.

Let $\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{n}\right)$. Automorphisms preserve element orders and conjugacy classes. Transpositions in $S_{n}$ form a unique conjugacy class of order-$2$ elements: their centralizer has order $2\cdot\left(n-2\right)!$, which differs from the centralizer orders of other order-$2$ elements (e.g. products of disjoint transpositions). Therefore $\varphi$ must send transpositions to transpositions.

Let $\varphi\left(12\right)=\left(ab\right)$. Composing $\varphi$ with a suitable inner automorphism, we may assume without loss of generality that $\varphi\left(12\right)=\left(12\right)$.

For $k=3,\dots,n$, the product of $\left(12\right)$ and $\left(1k\right)$, namely $\left(12\right)\left(1k\right)=\left(1k2\right)$, is a $3$-cycle (of order $3$). Hence $\varphi\left(1k\right)$ must be a transposition sharing exactly one letter with $\left(12\right)$ (otherwise the product with $\left(12\right)$ would not have order $3$). That is, $\varphi\left(1k\right)=\left(1,j_{k}\right)$ or $\varphi\left(1k\right)=\left(2,j_{k}\right)$.

If there exists $k$ such that $\varphi\left(1k\right)=\left(2,j_{k}\right)$, a further inner automorphism adjusts this uniformly to the form $\varphi\left(1k\right)=\left(1,\sigma\left(k\right)\right)$, where $\sigma\in S_{n-1}$ is a permutation of $\left\{3,\dots,n\right\}$.

Thus the action of $\varphi$ on the generators $\left(12\right),\left(13\right),\dots,\left(1n\right)$ equals the conjugation action of some $\sigma\in S_{n}$. By the result of **Problem 1**, these transpositions generate $S_{n}$, so $\varphi$ is the inner automorphism $\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma}$. Therefore $\varphi\in\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_{n}\right)$.

The above proof fails for $n=6$, because $S_{6}$ has outer automorphisms (sending transpositions to other order-$2$ elements such as squares of $6$-cycles; these order-$2$ elements have centralizers of the same order as transpositions), a phenomenon unique to $S_{6}$. When $n\ne 2,6$,

$$
\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{n}\right)=\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_{n}\right)\cong S_{n}.
$$

**$\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{2}\right)$:** $S_{2}=\left\{e,\left(12\right)\right\}$ is a cyclic group of order $2$; any automorphism must fix the identity, so $\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{2}\right)=\left\{\mathrm{id}\right\}$ is the trivial group.

**$\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{6}\right)$:** $S_{6}$ has outer automorphisms, and

$$
\left|\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{6}\right)\right|=2\cdot\left|S_{6}\right|=1440,
$$

i.e.

$$
\mathrm{Out}\left(S_{6}\right)=\mathrm{Aut}\left(S_{6}\right)/\mathrm{Inn}\left(S_{6}\right)\cong\mathbb Z_{2}.
$$

$\blacksquare$
