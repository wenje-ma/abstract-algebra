> **Theorem 3.4.5** (Structure Theorem) Every finitely generated module $M$ over a principal ideal domain $D$ admits a decomposition $M=Dy_1\oplus\cdots\oplus Dy_s$, where
> $$
> \operatorname{ann}\left(y_1\right)\supset\operatorname{ann}\left(y_2\right)\supset\cdots\supset\operatorname{ann}\left(y_s\right),
> $$
> and $y_j\ne0$. The $d_i$ corresponding to nonzero annihilators ($\operatorname{ann}\left(y_i\right)=\left(d_i\right)$) are called invariant factors; their factorizations into powers of irreducible elements are called elementary divisors.

> **Corollary 3.4.12** (Isomorphism Criterion) Let $M_1,M_2$ be finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain $D$. Then $M_1\cong M_2$ if and only if they have the same rank and the same invariant factors, which holds if and only if they have the same rank and the same elementary divisors.

> **Theorem 3.4.9** ($p$-Primary Decomposition) Let $M$ be a finitely generated torsion module over a principal ideal domain $D$. Then there exist finitely many pairwise non-associate irreducible elements $p_1,\cdots,p_m$ such that $M=M_{p_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{p_m}$, where
> $$
> M_p=\left\{x\in M\mid \exists e,\ p^ex=0\right\}
> $$
> is the $p$-primary component. In particular, if $\operatorname{ann}\left(y\right)=\left(ab\right)$ and $a,b$ are coprime, then $Dy=Dy_1\oplus Dy_2$, with $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_1\right)=\left(a\right)$ and $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_2\right)=\left(b\right)$.

> **Theorem 2.7.7** (Bézout Identity) Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain, $a,b\in R$, and let $d$ be a greatest common divisor of $a,b$. Then there exist $u,v\in R$ such that $d=ua+vb$.

### Problem 1

(Problem 3.4-4) Let $R=\mathbb Z\left[\alpha\right]$. Construct a finitely generated $R$-module $M$ that cannot be written as a direct sum of finitely many cyclic submodules. (The original statement omitted the condition on $\alpha$; following the standard interpretation we take $\alpha$ to be an indeterminate, i.e. $R=\mathbb Z\left[x\right]$, which is not a principal ideal domain.)

### Solution to Problem 1

Take $R=\mathbb Z\left[x\right]$, where $\alpha=x$ is an indeterminate. The ring $R$ is not a principal ideal domain: the ideal $I=\left(2,x\right)$ is not generated by a single element.

Now regard the ideal $I$ itself as an $R$-module. It is generated by $2$ and $x$, so it is a finitely generated $R$-module. We prove that $I$ cannot be written as a direct sum of finitely many cyclic submodules.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $I=N_1\oplus\cdots\oplus N_k$, where each $N_j$ is a cyclic submodule, say $N_j=Ry_j$. Since $I$ is an ideal of the integral domain $R$, as an $R$-module it is torsion-free (if $r\in R$ is nonzero and $a\in I$ is nonzero, then $ra\ne0$). Hence in each cyclic module $Ry_j\cong R/\operatorname{ann}\left(y_j\right)$ we have $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_j\right)=0$, i.e. $N_j\cong R$. It follows that $I\cong R^k$.

Now look at the rank. Tensoring with the field of fractions $\mathbb Q\left(x\right)$, we get $I\otimes_R\mathbb Q\left(x\right)=\mathbb Q\left(x\right)$, which has rank $1$. Hence $k=1$, i.e. $I\cong R$, and therefore $I$ is a principal ideal, $I=\left(g\right)$.

But $I=\left(2,x\right)$ is not principal: the only units of $R=\mathbb Z\left[x\right]$ are $\pm1$. If $I=\left(g\right)$, then $2=gf$ and $x=gh$. From $2=gf$ it follows that $f,g$ can only be the constants $\pm1,\pm2$; combined with $x=gh$ this is impossible ($x$ cannot divide $2$). More directly, the constant term of every element $2f+xg$ of $I$ is even, so $1\notin I$; hence $I\ne R=\left(1\right)$, and $I$ cannot be generated by $\pm2$ or by $x$ alone.

This is a contradiction. Hence $I$ cannot be written as a direct sum of finitely many cyclic submodules. This agrees with the fact that **Theorem 3.4.5** (Structure Theorem) holds only over principal ideal domains: without the principal-ideal-domain hypothesis, finitely generated modules generally do not decompose as direct sums of cyclic submodules. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 2

(Problem 3.4-5) Let $D=\mathbb Z\left[i\right]$ (the ring of Gaussian integers, which is a principal ideal domain), and let $K$ be the submodule of $D^3$ generated by the vector $\left(2-3i,6+9i,-18i\right)$. Find the invariant factors and elementary divisors of $D^3/K$. (The opening of the original statement is garbled; we complete it mathematically as above, where $i=\sqrt{-1}$.)

### Solution to Problem 2

Write

$$
v=\left(2-3i,6+9i,-18i\right)\in D^3,
$$

so that $K=Dv$. The quotient module $D^3/K$ is determined by the Smith normal form of the $1\times3$ matrix

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2-3i & 6+9i & -18i\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The first invariant factor is the greatest common divisor (up to associates) of the three entries of $A$.

We compute inside $D=\mathbb Z\left[i\right]$. Put $a=2-3i$; its norm is $N\left(a\right)=2^2+3^2=13$, so $a$ is an irreducible element of $D$ ($13=\left(2-3i\right)\left(2+3i\right)$). Also $b=6+9i=3\left(2+3i\right)$ and $c=-18i$, with $N\left(c\right)=18^2=324$.

Check whether $a=2-3i$ divides $b,c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{b}{a}&=\frac{3\left(2+3i\right)\left(2+3i\right)}{13}\\&=\frac{3\left(-5+12i\right)}{13}\\&\notin\mathbb Z\left[i\right],\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{c}{a}&=\frac{-18i\left(2+3i\right)}{13}\\&=\frac{54-36i}{13}\\&\notin\mathbb Z\left[i\right].\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $a\nmid b$ and $a\nmid c$. The only nontrivial divisors of $a$ are its associates (since $a$ is irreducible), so $a$ is not a common divisor of $b,c$. Symmetrically, $2+3i$ does not divide $a$ either. Therefore the greatest common divisor of $a,b,c$ is the unit $1$ in $D$.

Thus the Smith normal form of $A$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. By **Corollary 3.4.12** (Isomorphism Criterion),

$$
D^3/K\cong D/\left(1\right)\oplus D/\left(0\right)\oplus D/\left(0\right)\cong D^2.
$$

That is, $D^3/K$ has rank $2$ and zero torsion part. Hence its invariant factors (the torsion part $d_1,\cdots,d_k$) are empty, and its elementary divisors are also empty; the module is isomorphic to the free module $D^2$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 3

(Problem 3.4-6) Let $D$ be a principal ideal domain, let $M$ be a finitely generated torsion module over $D$, and for $a\in D$ define

$$
M\left(a\right)=\left\{x\in M\mid ax=0\right\}.
$$

Prove:

(1) $M\left(a\right)$ is a submodule of $M$, and if $a$ is invertible then $M\left(a\right)=\left\{0\right\}$;

(2) if $a\mid b$, then $M\left(a\right)\subset M\left(b\right)$;

(3) for $a,b\in D$,

$$
M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=M\left(\left(a,b\right)\right),
$$

where $\left(a,b\right)$ denotes a greatest common divisor of $a,b$;

(4) if $a,b$ are coprime, then $M\left(ab\right)=M\left(a\right)\oplus M\left(b\right)$.

### Solution to Problem 3

(1) $M\left(a\right)$ is nonempty (it contains $0$). If $x,y\in M\left(a\right)$, then $a\left(x-y\right)=ax-ay=0$; and for $r\in D$, $a\left(rx\right)=r\left(ax\right)=0$. Hence $M\left(a\right)$ is a submodule. If $a$ is invertible, multiplying $ax=0$ by $a^{-1}$ gives $x=0$, so $M\left(a\right)=\left\{0\right\}$.

(2) Write $b=ac$. If $x\in M\left(a\right)$, then $ax=0$, and so $bx=cax=0$, hence $x\in M\left(b\right)$. Therefore $M\left(a\right)\subset M\left(b\right)$.

(3) Let $d=\left(a,b\right)$ be a greatest common divisor. Since $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, by (2) we have $M\left(d\right)\subset M\left(a\right)$ and $M\left(d\right)\subset M\left(b\right)$, hence $M\left(d\right)\subset M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)$.

Conversely, if $x\in M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)$, then $ax=bx=0$. By **Theorem 2.7.7** (Bézout Identity), there exist $u,v\in D$ such that $d=ua+vb$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}dx&=\left(ua+vb\right)x\\&=uax+vbx\\&=0,\end{aligned}
$$

so $x\in M\left(d\right)$. Therefore

$$
M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=M\left(d\right)=M\left(\left(a,b\right)\right).
$$

(4) Suppose $a,b$ are coprime, i.e. $\left(a,b\right)=1$. By (2), $a\mid ab$ and $b\mid ab$, so $M\left(a\right)\subset M\left(ab\right)$ and $M\left(b\right)\subset M\left(ab\right)$, hence $M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)\subset M\left(ab\right)$.

Now compute the intersection. By (3),

$$
M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=M\left(\left(a,b\right)\right)=M\left(1\right)=\left\{0\right\}.
$$

Finally, show that $M\left(ab\right)\subset M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)$. Since $\left(a,b\right)=1$, by **Theorem 2.7.7** there exist $u,v$ such that $ua+vb=1$. For any $x\in M\left(ab\right)$, we have $abx=0$. Write

$$
\begin{aligned}x&=\left(ua+vb\right)x\\&=uax+vb x.\end{aligned}
$$

Set $x_1=vb x$ and $x_2=uax$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}ax_1&=avb x\\&=vab x\\&=0,\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}bx_2&=bua x\\&=uab x\\&=0,\end{aligned}
$$

so $x_1\in M\left(a\right)$ and $x_2\in M\left(b\right)$, and hence

$$
x=x_1+x_2\in M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right).
$$

In summary, $M\left(ab\right)=M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)$ and $M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=\left\{0\right\}$, so

$$
M\left(ab\right)=M\left(a\right)\oplus M\left(b\right).
$$

$\blacksquare$

### Problem 4

(Problem 3.4-10) Let $M$ be a torsion module over a principal ideal domain $D$. Prove that $M$ is simple if and only if $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p\right)$ for some prime element $p$; and that $M$ is indecomposable if and only if $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p^e\right)$ for some prime element $p$ and $e\in\mathbb N$. (The final expression in the original statement is garbled; we complete it as $\left(p^e\right)$ according to the textbook context.)

### Solution to Problem 4

**The simple case.**

Necessity. Let $M$ be a simple torsion module. By **Theorem 3.4.5** (Structure Theorem), $M=Dy_1\oplus\cdots\oplus Dy_s$. If $s\ge2$, then $Dy_1$ is a nontrivial submodule of $M$, contradicting simplicity. Hence $s=1$, $M=Dz$, with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(d\right)$.

If $d$ were not prime, then $d$ would have a nontrivial decomposition $d=ab$ with neither $a$ nor $b$ a unit. By **Theorem 3.4.9** ($p$-Primary Decomposition), $Dz=Dy_1\oplus Dy_2$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_1\right)=\left(a\right)$ and $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_2\right)=\left(b\right)$, both nonzero submodules, again contradicting simplicity. Hence $d=p$ is prime.

Sufficiency. Let $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p\right)$, $p$ prime. Then $M\cong D/\left(p\right)$. Since $p$ is prime, $\left(p\right)$ is a maximal ideal, $D/\left(p\right)$ is a field, and as a $D$-module it has only the zero submodule and itself. Hence $M$ is simple.

**The indecomposable case.**

Necessity. Let $M$ be indecomposable. By the structure theorem, $M=Dy_1\oplus\cdots\oplus Dy_s$. If $s\ge2$, it is already a direct sum of two nonzero submodules, a contradiction. Hence $s=1$, $M=Dz$, with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(d\right)$.

If $d$ contained two coprime non-unit factors, say $d=ab$ with $\left(a,b\right)=1$ and neither $a$ nor $b$ a unit, then by **Theorem 3.4.9** $Dz=Dz_1\oplus Dz_2$, both nonzero, a contradiction. Hence all irreducible factors of $d$ are associates, i.e. $d=p^e$ with $p$ prime and $e\in\mathbb N$.

Sufficiency. Let $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p^e\right)$. Then $M\cong D/\left(p^e\right)$. The submodules of $D/\left(p^e\right)$ correspond bijectively to the ideals of $D$ containing $\left(p^e\right)$, which are

$$
\left(p^e\right)\subset\left(p^{e-1}\right)\subset\cdots\subset\left(p\right)\subset D,
$$

forming a totally ordered chain. If $M=N_1\oplus N_2$ were a direct sum of two nonzero submodules, then both $N_1,N_2$ would be nonzero proper submodules in the above chain; they must be nested, hence $N_1\cap N_2\ne0$, contradicting the direct sum. Therefore $M$ is indecomposable.

(Note: if one allows the free cyclic module $Dz$ of rank $1$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=0$, it is likewise indecomposable, since the integral domain $D$ has no nontrivial direct-sum decomposition of ideals.) $\blacksquare$
