> **Theorem 2.7.7 (Bezout Identity)**<br>Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain, $a,b\in R$, and $d$ a greatest common divisor of $a,b$. Then there exist $u,v\in R$ such that $d=ua+vb$.

> **Theorem 3.4.5 (Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a PID)**<br>Every finitely generated module $M$ over a principal ideal domain $D$ has a decomposition $M=Dy_1\oplus\cdots\oplus Dy_s$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_1\right)\supset\operatorname{ann}\left(y_2\right)\supset\cdots\supset\operatorname{ann}\left(y_s\right)$ and $y_j\ne0$. The $d_i$ for which $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_i\right)=\left(d_i\right)$ with nonzero annihilator are called invariant factors; those factored into powers of irreducibles are called elementary divisors.

> **Theorem 3.4.9 ($p$-Primary Decomposition of Torsion Modules)**<br>Let $M$ be a finitely generated torsion module over a principal ideal domain $D$. Then there exist finitely many pairwise nonassociate irreducibles $p_1,\cdots,p_m$ such that $M=M_{p_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{p_m}$, where $M_p=\left\{x\in M\mid \exists e,p^ex=0\right\}$ is the $p$-primary component. In particular, if $\operatorname{ann}\left(y\right)=\left(ab\right)$ with $a,b$ coprime, then $Dy=Dy_1\oplus Dy_2$, $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_1\right)=\left(a\right)$, $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_2\right)=\left(b\right)$.

> **Corollary 3.4.12 (Isomorphism Criterion for Finitely Generated Modules)**<br>Let $M_1,M_2$ be finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain $D$. Then $M_1\cong M_2$ iff they have the same rank and invariant factors, iff they have the same rank and elementary divisors.

## 3.4-4

Let $R=\mathbb Z\left[\alpha\right]$. Construct a finitely generated $R$-module $M$ that cannot be written as a direct sum of finitely many cyclic submodules. (The original problem lost the condition on $\alpha$; here we take the standard choice that $\alpha$ is an indeterminate, i.e. $R=\mathbb Z\left[x\right]$, which is not a principal ideal domain.)

### Solution 3.4-4

Take $R=\mathbb Z\left[x\right]$, where $\alpha=x$ is an indeterminate. $R$ is not a principal ideal domain: the ideal $I=\left(2,x\right)$ is not generated by a single element.

Now view the ideal $I$ itself as an $R$-module. It is generated by $2$ and $x$, hence is a finitely generated $R$-module. We show that $I$ cannot be written as a direct sum of finitely many cyclic submodules.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $I=N_1\oplus\cdots\oplus N_k$, each $N_j$ cyclic, say $N_j=Ry_j$. Since $I$ is an ideal of the integral domain $R$, it is torsion-free as an $R$-module (if $r\in R$ is nonzero and $a\in I$ is nonzero, then $ra\ne0$). Hence in each cyclic module $Ry_j\cong R/\operatorname{ann}\left(y_j\right)$ we have $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_j\right)=0$, i.e. $N_j\cong R$. Thus $I\cong R^k$.

Now look at the rank. Tensor to the field of fractions $\mathbb Q\left(x\right)$: $I\otimes_R\mathbb Q\left(x\right)=\mathbb Q\left(x\right)$, of rank $1$. Hence $k=1$, i.e. $I\cong R$, so $I$ is principal, $I=\left(g\right)$.

But $I=\left(2,x\right)$ is not principal: the only units of $R=\mathbb Z\left[x\right]$ are $\pm1$. If $I=\left(g\right)$, then $2=gf$ and $x=gh$. From $2=gf$, $f,g$ must be constants $\pm1,\pm2$; combined with $x=gh$, this is impossible ($x$ does not divide $2$). More directly, every element $2f+xg$ of $I$ has even constant term, so $1\notin I$; hence $I\ne R=\left(1\right)$, and it cannot be generated by $\pm2$ or by $x$ alone.

Contradiction. Thus $I$ cannot be written as a direct sum of finitely many cyclic submodules. This agrees with the fact that **Theorem 3.4.5 (Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a PID)** holds only over a principal ideal domain: without the PID hypothesis, a finitely generated module need not decompose into cyclic submodules. $\blacksquare$

## 3.4-5

Let $D=\mathbb Z\left[i\right]$ (the Gaussian integers, a principal ideal domain), and let $K$ be the submodule of $D^3$ generated by the vector $\left(2-3i,6+9i,-18i\right)$. Find the invariant factors and elementary divisors of $D^3/K$. (The beginning of the original problem is damaged; it is completed above in the mathematically valid form, with $i=\sqrt{-1}$.)

### Solution 3.4-5

Set $v=\left(2-3i,6+9i,-18i\right)\in D^3$ and $K=Dv$. The quotient $D^3/K$ is determined by the Smith normal form of the $1\times3$ matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}2-3i & 6+9i & -18i\end{bmatrix}$. The first invariant factor is the gcd (up to associates) of the three entries of $A$.

Compute in $D=\mathbb Z\left[i\right]$. Set $a=2-3i$; its norm $N\left(a\right)=2^2+3^2=13$, so $a$ is irreducible in $D$ ($13=\left(2-3i\right)\left(2+3i\right)$). Also $b=6+9i=3\left(2+3i\right)$, $c=-18i$, and $N\left(c\right)=18^2=324$.

Check whether $a=2-3i$ divides $b,c$:

$$
\frac{b}{a}=\frac{3\left(2+3i\right)\left(2+3i\right)}{13}=\frac{3\left(-5+12i\right)}{13}\notin\mathbb Z\left[i\right],
$$

$$
\frac{c}{a}=\frac{-18i\left(2+3i\right)}{13}=\frac{54-36i}{13}\notin\mathbb Z\left[i\right].
$$

Thus $a\nmid b$ and $a\nmid c$. Since the only nontrivial factors of $a$ are its associates ($a$ is irreducible), $a$ is not a common divisor of $b,c$. Similarly $2+3i$ does not divide $a$. Hence the gcd of $a,b,c$ is the unit $1$ in $D$.

Therefore the Smith normal form of $A$ is $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$. By **Corollary 3.4.12 (Isomorphism Criterion for Finitely Generated Modules)**,

$$
D^3/K\cong D/\left(1\right)\oplus D/\left(0\right)\oplus D/\left(0\right)\cong D^2.
$$

Thus $D^3/K$ has rank $2$ and zero torsion. Its invariant factors (the torsion part $d_1,\cdots,d_k$) are empty, and its elementary divisors are empty; the module is isomorphic to the free module $D^2$. $\blacksquare$

## 3.4-6

Let $D$ be a principal ideal domain and $M$ a finitely generated torsion $D$-module. For $a\in D$, set $M\left(a\right)=\left\{x\in M\mid ax=0\right\}$. Prove:

(1) $M\left(a\right)$ is a submodule of $M$, and if $a$ is invertible then $M\left(a\right)=\left\{0\right\}$;

(2) if $a\mid b$, then $M\left(a\right)\subset M\left(b\right)$;

(3) for $a,b\in D$, $M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=M\left(\left(a,b\right)\right)$, where $\left(a,b\right)$ is a gcd of $a,b$;

(4) if $a,b$ are coprime, then $M\left(ab\right)=M\left(a\right)\oplus M\left(b\right)$.

### Solution 3.4-6

(1) $M\left(a\right)$ is nonempty (contains $0$). If $x,y\in M\left(a\right)$, then $a\left(x-y\right)=ax-ay=0$; for $r\in D$, $a\left(rx\right)=r\left(ax\right)=0$. Hence $M\left(a\right)$ is a submodule. If $a$ is invertible, multiplying $ax=0$ by $a^{-1}$ gives $x=0$, so $M\left(a\right)=\left\{0\right\}$.

(2) Write $b=ac$. If $x\in M\left(a\right)$ and $ax=0$, then $bx=cax=0$, so $x\in M\left(b\right)$. Hence $M\left(a\right)\subset M\left(b\right)$.

(3) Let $d=\left(a,b\right)$ be a gcd. Since $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, by (2) we have $M\left(d\right)\subset M\left(a\right)$ and $M\left(d\right)\subset M\left(b\right)$, so $M\left(d\right)\subset M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)$.

Conversely, if $x\in M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)$, then $ax=bx=0$. By **Theorem 2.7.7 (Bezout Identity)**, there exist $u,v\in D$ with $d=ua+vb$. Then $dx=\left(ua+vb\right)x=uax+vbx=0$, so $x\in M\left(d\right)$. Hence $M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=M\left(d\right)=M\left(\left(a,b\right)\right)$.

(4) Let $a,b$ be coprime, i.e. $\left(a,b\right)=1$. By (2), $a\mid ab$ and $b\mid ab$, so $M\left(a\right)\subset M\left(ab\right)$ and $M\left(b\right)\subset M\left(ab\right)$, hence $M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)\subset M\left(ab\right)$.

Now the intersection. By (3), $M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=M\left(\left(a,b\right)\right)=M\left(1\right)=\left\{0\right\}$ (using (1)).

Finally show $M\left(ab\right)\subset M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)$. Since $\left(a,b\right)=1$, by **Theorem 2.7.7 (Bezout Identity)** there exist $u,v$ with $ua+vb=1$. For any $x\in M\left(ab\right)$, $abx=0$. Write $x=\left(ua+vb\right)x=uax+vb x$. Set $x_1=vb x$ and $x_2=uax$. Then $ax_1=avb x=vab x=0$ and $bx_2=bua x=uab x=0$, so $x_1\in M\left(a\right)$ and $x_2\in M\left(b\right)$, hence $x=x_1+x_2\in M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)$.

Thus $M\left(ab\right)=M\left(a\right)+M\left(b\right)$ and $M\left(a\right)\cap M\left(b\right)=\left\{0\right\}$, so $M\left(ab\right)=M\left(a\right)\oplus M\left(b\right)$. $\blacksquare$

## 3.4-10

Let $M$ be a torsion module over a principal ideal domain $D$. Prove that $M$ is simple iff $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p\right)$ for a prime element $p$; and $M$ is indecomposable iff $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p^e\right)$ for a prime $p$ and $e\in\mathbb N$. (The final formula in the original problem is damaged; it is completed above as $\left(p^e\right)$ according to the textbook context.)

### Solution 3.4-10

**Simple module case.**

Necessity. Let $M$ be a simple torsion module. By **Theorem 3.4.5 (Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a PID)**, $M=Dy_1\oplus\cdots\oplus Dy_s$. If $s\ge2$, then $Dy_1$ is a nontrivial submodule of $M$, contradicting simplicity. Hence $s=1$, $M=Dz$, $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(d\right)$.

If $d$ is not prime, then $d$ has a nontrivial factorization $d=ab$ with $a,b$ both nonunits. By **Theorem 3.4.9 ($p$-Primary Decomposition of Torsion Modules)**, $Dz=Dy_1\oplus Dy_2$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_1\right)=\left(a\right)$ and $\operatorname{ann}\left(y_2\right)=\left(b\right)$, both nonzero submodules, again contradicting simplicity. Hence $d=p$ is prime.

Sufficiency. Let $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p\right)$, $p$ prime. Then $M\cong D/\left(p\right)$. Since $p$ is prime, $\left(p\right)$ is a maximal ideal, $D/\left(p\right)$ is a field, and as a $D$-module it has only the zero submodule and itself. Hence $M$ is simple.

**Indecomposable module case.**

Necessity. Let $M$ be indecomposable. By the structure theorem, $M=Dy_1\oplus\cdots\oplus Dy_s$. If $s\ge2$, it is already a direct sum of two nonzero submodules, a contradiction. Hence $s=1$, $M=Dz$, $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(d\right)$.

If $d$ has two coprime nonunit factors, i.e. $d=ab$ with $\left(a,b\right)=1$ and $a,b$ both nonunits, then by **Theorem 3.4.9 ($p$-Primary Decomposition of Torsion Modules)**, $Dz=Dz_1\oplus Dz_2$, both nonzero, a contradiction. Hence all irreducible factors of $d$ are associate, i.e. $d=p^e$ with $p$ prime and $e\in\mathbb N$.

Sufficiency. Let $M=Dz$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=\left(p^e\right)$. Then $M\cong D/\left(p^e\right)$. The submodules of $D/\left(p^e\right)$ correspond bijectively to the ideals of $D$ containing $\left(p^e\right)$, namely $\left(p^e\right)\subset\left(p^{e-1}\right)\subset\cdots\subset\left(p\right)\subset D$, a single chain. If $M=N_1\oplus N_2$ were a direct sum of two nonzero submodules, then $N_1,N_2$ would both be nonzero proper submodules in this chain, hence comparable, so $N_1\cap N_2\ne0$, contradicting the direct sum. Thus $M$ is indecomposable.

(Note: if one allows a free cyclic module $Dz$ of rank $1$ with $\operatorname{ann}\left(z\right)=0$, it is likewise indecomposable, since the integral domain $D$ has no nontrivial direct-sum decomposition of ideals.) $\blacksquare$
