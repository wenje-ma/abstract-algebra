> **Corollary 3.3.10** (Uniqueness of the Rank of a Free Module) Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity. Then $R^m\cong R^n$ if and only if $m=n$, where $R^m$ denotes the external direct sum of $m$ copies of $R$.

> **Theorem 3.3.6** (Characterization of a Basis)
> 
> Let $M$ be a module over a ring $R$ with identity, and let $u_1,\cdots,u_m$ be elements of $M$. Then $M$ is free and $u_1,\cdots,u_m$ form a basis if and only if
> 
> (1) $u_1,\cdots,u_m$ generate $M$;
> 
> (2) $u_1,\cdots,u_m$ are linearly independent.

> **Basic Knowledge** (Determinant) A square matrix $A$ over a commutative ring $R$ with identity is invertible if and only if its determinant $\det A$ is a unit in $R$.

### Problem 1

(Problem 3.3-6) Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity, and suppose every finitely generated $R$-module is free. Prove that $R$ is a field.

### Solution to Problem 1

It suffices to show that every nonzero element of $R$ is a unit. Take any $a\in R$ with $a\ne0$. Consider the quotient module $R/\left(a\right)$, which is generated by $\overline 1=1+\left(a\right)$ and is therefore a finitely generated $R$-module. By hypothesis, $R/\left(a\right)$ is a free module.

We claim that $R/\left(a\right)=0$. Otherwise it would be a nonzero free module, and $\overline 1$ would be one of its generators. By **Theorem 3.3.6** (Characterization of a Basis), if $\left\{\overline 1\right\}$ were a basis, then its annihilator would be zero, i.e. $r\overline 1=0\Rightarrow r=0$. But $a\overline 1=a+\left(a\right)=0$ with $a\ne0$, a contradiction. Hence $R/\left(a\right)=0$.

The equality $R/\left(a\right)=0$ is equivalent to $\left(a\right)=R$, i.e. $1\in\left(a\right)$; in other words, there exists $b\in R$ such that $ab=1$. Hence $a$ is a unit.

Since $a\ne0$ was arbitrary, every nonzero element of $R$ is a unit, and therefore $R$ is a field. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 2

(Problem 3.3-7) Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity, and let $\phi$ be an endomorphism of the free module $R^m$. Prove that if $\phi$ is an epimorphism, then $\phi$ is an isomorphism.

### Solution to Problem 2

Take the standard basis $e_1,\cdots,e_m$ of $R^m$, and let $A\in R^{m\times m}$ be the matrix of the endomorphism $\phi$ in this basis.

Since $\phi$ is an epimorphism, for each basis vector $e_j\in R^m$ there exists $x_j\in R^m$ such that $\phi\left(x_j\right)=e_j$. Arrange $x_1,\cdots,x_m$ as the columns of a matrix $B$; then

$$
AB=I_m,
$$

where $I_m$ is the $m\times m$ identity matrix. Taking determinants on both sides and using multiplicativity of the determinant,

$$
\det\left(A\right)\det\left(B\right)=\det\left(I_m\right)=1.
$$

Hence $\det A$ is a unit in $R$. By the basic fact on determinants, $A$ is invertible, i.e. there exists $A^{-1}$ such that $AA^{-1}=I_m$. Thus $\phi$ is both an epimorphism and a monomorphism ($\ker\phi=0$), hence bijective, and so $\phi$ is an isomorphism of modules. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 3

(Problem 3.3-8) With the same hypotheses as in the previous problem, if $R$ is a field and $\phi$ is a monomorphism, prove that $\phi$ is an isomorphism. Does this conclusion hold for a general commutative ring with identity?

### Solution to Problem 3

When $R$ is a field, $R^m$ is an $m$-dimensional vector space. That $\phi$ is a monomorphism means that the kernel of the linear transformation $\phi$ is the zero subspace. By linear algebra, an injective linear transformation on a finite-dimensional vector space is necessarily surjective, hence an isomorphism. Equivalently, in matrix terms, a monomorphism corresponds to a matrix $A$ whose columns are linearly independent, i.e. $\operatorname{rank}A=m$; hence the square matrix $A$ has full rank and is invertible, and $\phi$ is an isomorphism.

This conclusion does not hold for a general commutative ring with identity.

Counterexample. Take $R=\mathbb Z$ (a commutative ring with identity) and $m=1$, and define $\phi:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$ by

$$
\phi\left(n\right)=2n.
$$

Then $\phi$ is an endomorphism of $\mathbb Z$. It is a monomorphism: if $2n=0$, then $n=0$ in the integral domain $\mathbb Z$. But it is not an epimorphism: $1\notin 2\mathbb Z$, i.e. there is no $n$ such that $2n=1$. Hence $\phi$ is injective but not bijective, and is not an isomorphism.

Therefore, over a general commutative ring with identity, a monic endomorphism of a free module need not be an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$
