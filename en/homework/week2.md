> **Definition 1.2.1** (Subgroup) Let $H$ be a nonempty subset of a group $G$. If $H$ itself forms a group under the operation of $G$, then $H$ is called a subgroup of $G$, denoted $H<G$.

> **Theorem 1.2.2** (Subgroup Criterion) Let $H$ be a nonempty subset of a group $G$. Then $H<G$ if and only if $a,b\in H$ for any $ab^{-1}\in H$.

> **Theorem 1.2.22** (Lagrange's Theorem) Let $G$ be a finite group and $H<G$. Then $\left|G\right|=\left[G:H\right]\cdot\left|H\right|$, where $\left[G:H\right]$ is the index of $H$ in $G$. Hence the order of the subgroup $H$ divides the order of the group $G$; in particular, the order of any element also divides $\left|G\right|$.

> > **Corollary 1.2.23** (Multiplicativity of Index) Let $G$ be a finite group and $K<H<G$. Then
> $$
> \left[G:K\right]=\left[G:H\right]\cdot\left[H:K\right].
> $$

> **Basic Knowledge** (Order of an Element) Let $G$ be a group and $a\in G$. Then $\left\langle a\right\rangle=\left\{a^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb Z\right\}$ is a subgroup of $G$, called the subgroup generated by $a$. The order of this subgroup is called the order of the element $a$.

> **Basic Knowledge** (Least Common Multiple and Greatest Common Divisor) For positive integers $m,n$, $\left(m,n\right)$ denotes the greatest common divisor and $\left[m,n\right]$ the least common multiple, satisfying $\left(m,n\right)\left[m,n\right]=mn$.

### Problem 1

(Problem 1.2-8) Let $a,b$ be elements of orders $G$ in a group $m,n$, respectively, with $ab=ba$.

(1) If $\left(m,n\right)=1$, prove that the order of $ab$ is $mn$;

(2) If $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\}$, prove that the order of $ab$ is $\left[m,n\right]$;

(3) If $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\ne\left\{e\right\}$, discuss the order of $ab$. Does $G$ necessarily contain an element of order $\left[m,n\right]$?

### Solution to Problem 1

(1) Since $ab=ba$, for any integer $k$ we have $\left(ab\right)^{k}=a^{k}b^{k}$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}\left(ab\right)^{mn}&=a^{mn}b^{mn}\\&=\left(a^{m}\right)^{n}\left(b^{n}\right)^{m}\\&=e,\end{aligned}
$$

so the order of $ab$ divides $mn$.

Conversely, if $\left(ab\right)^{k}=e$, then $a^{k}=b^{-k}$. The left-hand side lies in $\left\langle a\right\rangle$ and the right-hand side in $\left\langle b\right\rangle$, so $a^{k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle$. By **Theorem 1.2.22** (Lagrange's Theorem), $\left|\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\right|$ divides both $\left|\left\langle a\right\rangle\right|=m$ and $\left|\left\langle b\right\rangle\right|=n$, and hence divides $\left(m,n\right)=1$, i.e. $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\}$. Therefore $a^{k}=e$, $b^{k}=e$, and thus $m\mid k$, $n\mid k$. Since $\left(m,n\right)=1$, $mn\mid k$.

In summary, the order of $ab$ is $mn$.

(2) Let $d=\left[m,n\right]$. Since $m\mid d$, $n\mid d$,

$$
\begin{aligned}\left(ab\right)^{d}&=a^{d}b^{d}\\&=e,\end{aligned}
$$

so the order of $ab$ divides $d$.

Conversely, if $\left(ab\right)^{k}=e$, then

$$
a^{k}=b^{-k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\},
$$

so $a^{k}=e$, $b^{k}=e$. Hence $m\mid k$, $n\mid k$, and therefore $\left[m,n\right]\mid k$.

Thus the order of $ab$ is $\left[m,n\right]$.

(3) When $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\ne\left\{e\right\}$, the order of $ab$ still divides $\left[m,n\right]$, but may be strictly smaller than $\left[m,n\right]$.

Example: take $G=\mathbb Z_{4}\times\mathbb Z_{4}$, $a=\left(1,0\right)$, $b=\left(1,2\right)$. Then both $a,b$ have order $4$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle a\right\rangle&=\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(1,0\right),\left(2,0\right),\left(3,0\right)\right\},\
\left\langle b\right\rangle&=\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(1,2\right),\left(2,0\right),\left(3,2\right)\right\}
\end{aligned},
$$

whose intersection is $\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(2,0\right)\right\}\ne\left\{e\right\}$. Meanwhile $ab=\left(2,2\right)$ satisfies $2ab=\left(0,0\right)$, so the order of $ab$ is $2<\left[4,4\right]=4$.

As to whether $G$ necessarily contains an element of order $\left[m,n\right]$, the answer is yes. In fact, from $ab=ba$ the subgroup $H=\left\langle a,b\right\rangle$ is abelian. In the abelian group $H$, write $m=\prod p^{\alpha_{p}}$, $n=\prod p^{\beta_{p}}$ as prime factorizations. For each prime $p$, if $\alpha_{p}\ge\beta_{p}$, take $a^{m/p^{\alpha_{p}}}$, whose order is $p^{\alpha_{p}}$; if $\beta_{p}>\alpha_{p}$, take $b^{n/p^{\beta_{p}}}$, whose order is $p^{\beta_{p}}$. Multiply all these elements together (the product is well-defined since $H$ is abelian); the resulting element has order $\prod p^{\max\left(\alpha_{p},\beta_{p}\right)}=\left[m,n\right]$. Hence $G$ necessarily contains an element of order $\left[m,n\right]$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 2

(Problem 1.2-13) Let $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$ be the set of all $1$ integer matrices with determinant $n$.

(1) Prove that $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$ is a subgroup of $GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$;

(2) Let $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Prove that any two of these elements generate $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

### Solution to Problem 2

(1) $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$ is nonempty, since the entries of the identity matrix $I_{n}$ are all integers and $\det I_{n}=1$.

For any $A,B\in SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$, the entries of $A,B$ are all integers, so the entries of $AB^{-1}$ are also integers, and

$$
\begin{aligned}\det\left(AB^{-1}\right)&=\det A\cdot\det\left(B^{-1}\right)\\&=\det A/\det B\\&=1/1\\&=1.\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $AB^{-1}\in SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$. By **Theorem 1.2.2** (Subgroup Criterion), $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)<GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$.

(2) First verify that $S,T,R,U$ all belong to $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$: each matrix has integer entries and determinant $1$.

We first show $\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$. This is the standard generator result for $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$: for any $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$, left-multiplying by $T^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (corresponding to the row operation: add $k$ times the second row to the first) and $U^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\k&1\end{pmatrix}$ (corresponding to adding $k$ times the first row to the second), together with the Euclidean algorithm, reduces $A$ to diagonal form; since the determinant is $1$, the diagonal entries must be $\pm1$, and $S$ expresses $-I$. Hence $\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

We now show that each of the remaining pairs also equals $\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$.

**$R=ST$** A direct computation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}ST&=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\\&=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\\&=R.\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $T=S^{-1}R$, and therefore

$$
\left\langle S,R\right\rangle=\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right).
$$
Similarly, $S=RT^{-1}$, so

$$
\left\langle T,R\right\rangle=\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right).
$$

**Relation between $U$ and $S$** Compute $S^{-1}TS$:

$$
\begin{aligned}S^{-1}TS&=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\\&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\\&=U^{-1}.\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $T=SU^{-1}S^{-1}$, so $\left\langle S,U\right\rangle\supseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$. Also $U=S^{-1}T^{-1}S\in\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$, so

$$
\left\langle S,U\right\rangle=\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right).
$$

**$\left\langle T,U\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$** $T$ and $U$ are precisely the elementary matrices $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. By the Euclidean algorithm above, using only these two elementary transformations reduces any element of $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$ to $\pm I$, so $\left\langle T,U\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

**$\left\langle R,U\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$** From $R=ST$ and $U^{-1}=S^{-1}TS$, substituting gives

$$
\begin{aligned}U^{-1}&=S^{-1}\left(S^{-1}R\right)S\\&=S^{-2}RS\\&=-IRS\\&=-RS,\end{aligned}
$$

where $S^{2}=-I$. Hence $S=-R^{-1}U^{-1}$. Also $R^{3}=-I$ (a direct check gives $R^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$), so $-I=R^{3}\in\left\langle R,U\right\rangle$, and thus

$$
S=R^{3}R^{-1}U^{-1}=R^{2}U^{-1}\in\left\langle R,U\right\rangle.
$$
Then $T=S^{-1}R\in\left\langle R,U\right\rangle$, so

$$
\left\langle R,U\right\rangle\supseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right).
$$

The reverse inclusion is clear, so $\left\langle R,U\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

In summary, any two of $S,T,R,U$ generate $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 3

(Problem 1.2-20) Let $H_{1},H_{2}$ be two subgroups of a finite group $G$. Prove that

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]\le\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right].
$$
Moreover, if $\left[G:H_{1}\right]$ and $\left[G:H_{2}\right]$ are coprime, then

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]
$$

and $G=H_{1}H_{2}$.

### Solution to Problem 3

First prove the inequality. Consider the map

$$
\varphi:G/\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)\to G/H_{1}\times G/H_{2}, g\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)\mapsto\left(gH_{1},gH_{2}\right).
$$

**Well-definedness** If

$$
g\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)=g'\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right),
$$

then $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}\subseteq H_{1}$ and $\subseteq H_{2}$, so $gH_{1}=g'H_{1}$, $gH_{2}=g'H_{2}$.

**Injectivity** If

$$
\left(gH_{1},gH_{2}\right)=\left(g'H_{1},g'H_{2}\right),
$$
then $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}$ and $g^{-1}g'\in H_{2}$, so $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}$, and thus

$$
g\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)=g'\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right).
$$

Therefore $\varphi$ is injective, and hence

$$
\begin{aligned}\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]&=\left|G/\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)\right|\\&\le\left|G/H_{1}\right|\cdot\left|G/H_{2}\right|\\&=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right].\end{aligned}
$$

Now prove equality in the coprime case. By **Corollary 1.2.23** (Multiplicativity of Index),

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[H_{1}:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right].
$$

Since $H_{1}\cap H_{2}<H_{1}<G$ and $H_{1}\cap H_{2}<H_{2}<G$, we have $\left[G:H_{1}\right]\mid\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]$ and $\left[G:H_{2}\right]\mid\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]$. Since $\left[G:H_{1}\right]$ and $\left[G:H_{2}\right]$ are coprime, their product

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]\mid\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right].
$$

Combining with the already-proved inequality

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]\le\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right],
$$

we get

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right].
$$

Finally prove $G=H_{1}H_{2}$. By the order formula for a product of subgroups,

$$
\left|H_{1}H_{2}\right|=\frac{\left|H_{1}\right|\left|H_{2}\right|}{\left|H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right|}.
$$

By **Theorem 1.2.22** (Lagrange's Theorem), $\left|H_{1}\right|=\left|G\right|/\left[G:H_{1}\right]$, $\left|H_{2}\right|=\left|G\right|/\left[G:H_{2}\right]$, and

$$
\left|H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right|=\left|G\right|/\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left|G\right|/\left(\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]\right).
$$

Substituting gives

$$
\begin{aligned}\left|H_{1}H_{2}\right|&=\frac{\left(\left|G\right|/\left[G:H_{1}\right]\right)\cdot\left(\left|G\right|/\left[G:H_{2}\right]\right)}{\left|G\right|/\left(\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]\right)}\\&=\left|G\right|.\end{aligned}
$$

Since $H_{1}H_{2}\subseteq G$ and both have the same number of elements, $G=H_{1}H_{2}$. $\blacksquare$
