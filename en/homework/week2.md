> **Basic Knowledge: Order of an Element and Generated Subgroup**<br>Let $a\in G$; then $\left\langle a\right\rangle=\left\{a^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb Z\right\}$ is a subgroup of $G$, called the subgroup generated by $a$; the order of this subgroup is called the order of the element $a$. If the order of $a$ is $m$, then $a^{m}=e$, and for every integer $k$ we have $a^{k}=e$ if and only if $m\mid k$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Distribution of Powers in Commutative Groups**<br>If $ab=ba$, then $\left(ab\right)^{k}=a^{k}b^{k}$ for every integer $k$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Order of a Power of an Element**<br>If the order of $a$ is $m$, then the order of $a^{k}$ is $\frac{m}{\left(m,k\right)}$, where $\left(m,k\right)$ is the greatest common divisor of $m$ and $k$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple**<br>For positive integers $m,n$, let $\left(m,n\right)$ be the greatest common divisor and $\left[m,n\right]$ the least common multiple; then $\left(m,n\right)\left[m,n\right]=mn$; $m\mid k$ and $n\mid k$ if and only if $\left[m,n\right]\mid k$; if $\left(m,n\right)=1$, then $m\mid k$ and $n\mid k$ if and only if $mn\mid k$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Prime Factorization and Highest Powers**<br>Every positive integer $m$ decomposes uniquely as $m=\prod_{p}p^{\alpha_p}$; for $m=\prod_{p}p^{\alpha_p}$, $n=\prod_{p}p^{\beta_p}$ we have $\left[m,n\right]=\prod_{p}p^{\max\left(\alpha_p,\beta_p\right)}$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Order of a Product of Commuting Elements of Coprime Order**<br>If $x,y$ commute and $o\left(x\right),o\left(y\right)$ are coprime, then $o\left(xy\right)=o\left(x\right)o\left(y\right)$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Adjugate Matrices and Determinants of Integer Matrices**<br>If all entries of a square matrix $B$ are integers and $\left|B\right|=1$, then all entries of $B^{-1}$ are integers; moreover $\left|AB^{-1}\right|=\left|A\right|\left|B^{-1}\right|=\frac{\left|A\right|}{\left|B\right|}$.

> **Basic Knowledge: Elementary Row Operations and the Euclidean Algorithm**<br>Multiplying on the left by $\begin{bmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ is equivalent to adding $k$ times one row to another; by the Euclidean algorithm, repeatedly applying these two kinds of elementary row operations reduces any integer matrix to diagonal form; if the resulting diagonal matrix has determinant $1$, then its diagonal entries are $\pm1$, and $\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$ can be expressed in terms of $\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ (since its square is $-I$).

> **Basic Knowledge: Order of a Product of Finite Subsets**<br>Let $H,K$ be finite subgroups of a group $G$; then $\left|HK\right|=\frac{\left|H\right|\left|K\right|}{\left|H\cap K\right|}$: indeed the map $H\times K\to HK$, $\left(h,k\right)\mapsto hk$ is surjective, and $\left(h_1,k_1\right),\left(h_2,k_2\right)$ have the same image if and only if $h_1^{-1}h_2=k_1k_2^{-1}\in H\cap K$, so every image has exactly $\left|H\cap K\right|$ preimages.

> **Definition 1.2.1 (Subgroup)**<br>Let $H$ be a nonempty subset of a group $G$. If $H$ forms a group under the operation of $G$, then $H$ is called a subgroup of $G$, denoted $H<G$.

> **Theorem 1.2.2 (Subgroup Criterion)**<br>Let $H$ be a nonempty subset of a group $G$. Then the following conditions are equivalent: (1) $H$ is a subgroup of $G$; (2) for all $a,b\in H$, $ab\in H$ and $a^{-1}\in H$; (3) for all $a,b\in H$, $ab^{-1}\in H$.

> **Lemma 1.2.12 (Coset Coincidence Criterion)**<br>Let $H$ be a subgroup of a group $G$ and $a,b\in G$. Then $aH$ and $bH$ are either disjoint or identical, and $aH=bH$ if and only if $a^{-1}b\in H$.

> **Theorem 1.2.22 (Lagrange's Theorem)**<br>Let $G$ be a finite group and $H<G$. Then $\left|G\right|=\left[G:H\right]\cdot\left|H\right|$, where $\left[G:H\right]$ is the index of $H$ in $G$. Hence the order of the subgroup $H$ is a divisor of the order of the group $G$; in particular, the order of any element is also a divisor of $\left|G\right|$.

> **Corollary 1.2.23 (Multiplicativity of the Index)**<br>Let $G$ be a finite group, $K<G$, $H<K$. Then $\left[G:H\right]=\left[G:K\right]\cdot\left[K:H\right]$.

## 1.2-8

Let $a,b$ be elements of orders $m,n$ respectively in a group $G$, with $ab=ba$.

(1) Prove that the order of $ab$ is $mn$ if $\left(m,n\right)=1$;

(2) Prove that the order of $ab$ is $\left[m,n\right]$ if $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\}$;

(3) If $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\ne\left\{e\right\}$, discuss the order of $ab$. Does $G$ necessarily contain an element of order $\left[m,n\right]$?

### Solution 1.2-8

**(1) Prove that the order of $ab$ is $mn$.** By **Basic Knowledge: Distribution of Powers in Commutative Groups**, since $ab=ba$, we have $\left(ab\right)^{k}=a^{k}b^{k}$ for every integer $k$. Hence

$$
\left(ab\right)^{mn}=a^{mn}b^{mn}=\left(a^{m}\right)^{n}\left(b^{n}\right)^{m}=e,
$$

so by **Basic Knowledge: Order of an Element and Generated Subgroup**, the order of $ab$ divides $mn$.

Conversely, suppose $\left(ab\right)^{k}=e$. Then by $\left(ab\right)^{k}=a^{k}b^{k}$ we get $a^{k}b^{k}=e$, i.e. $a^{k}=b^{-k}$. The left side $a^{k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle$ and the right side $b^{-k}\in\left\langle b\right\rangle$, so $a^{k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle$. By **Theorem 1.2.22 (Lagrange's Theorem)**, $\left|\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\right|$ divides both $\left|\left\langle a\right\rangle\right|=m$ and $\left|\left\langle b\right\rangle\right|=n$, hence divides $\left(m,n\right)=1$; therefore $\left|\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\right|=1$, i.e. $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\}$. Hence $a^{k}=b^{-k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\}$, i.e. $a^{k}=e$ and $b^{k}=e$. By **Basic Knowledge: Order of an Element and Generated Subgroup**, $m\mid k$ and $n\mid k$. Since $\left(m,n\right)=1$, by **Basic Knowledge: Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple**, $mn\mid k$.

In summary, the order of $ab$ divides $mn$ and $mn$ divides the order of $ab$; hence the order of $ab$ is $mn$.

**(2) Prove that the order of $ab$ is $\left[m,n\right]$.** Write $d=\left[m,n\right]$. By **Basic Knowledge: Distribution of Powers in Commutative Groups**, $\left(ab\right)^{d}=a^{d}b^{d}$. Since $m\mid d$ and $n\mid d$, we have $a^{d}=\left(a^{m}\right)^{\frac{d}{m}}=e$ and $b^{d}=\left(b^{n}\right)^{\frac{d}{n}}=e$, so $\left(ab\right)^{d}=e$; by **Basic Knowledge: Order of an Element and Generated Subgroup**, the order of $ab$ divides $d$.

Conversely, suppose $\left(ab\right)^{k}=e$. Then by $\left(ab\right)^{k}=a^{k}b^{k}$ we get $a^{k}=b^{-k}$. Since $b^{-k}\in\left\langle b\right\rangle$ and $a^{k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle$, we have $a^{k}\in\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{e\right\}$, i.e. $a^{k}=e$, hence $b^{k}=e$. By **Basic Knowledge: Order of an Element and Generated Subgroup**, $m\mid k$ and $n\mid k$, and by **Basic Knowledge: Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple**, $\left[m,n\right]\mid k$.

In summary, the order of $ab$ divides $d$ and $d$ divides the order of $ab$; hence the order of $ab$ is $\left[m,n\right]$.

**(3) Discuss the order of $ab$.** When $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle\ne\left\{e\right\}$, the argument of (2) shows that the order of $ab$ still divides $\left[m,n\right]$, but may be strictly smaller than $\left[m,n\right]$.

Example: take $G=\mathbb Z_4\times\mathbb Z_4$, $a=\left(1,0\right)$, $b=\left(1,2\right)$. Then both $a,b$ have order $4$, and

$$
\left\langle a\right\rangle=\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(1,0\right),\left(2,0\right),\left(3,0\right)\right\},\quad\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(1,2\right),\left(2,0\right),\left(3,2\right)\right\},
$$

whose intersection is $\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap\left\langle b\right\rangle=\left\{\left(0,0\right),\left(2,0\right)\right\}\ne\left\{e\right\}$. Moreover $ab=\left(2,2\right)$ and $2ab=\left(0,0\right)$, so the order of $ab$ is $2<4=\left[4,4\right]$.

As for whether $G$ necessarily contains an element of order $\left[m,n\right]$, the answer is yes. Since $ab=ba$, the subgroup $H=\left\langle a,b\right\rangle$ is commutative. Let $m=\prod_{p}p^{\alpha_p}$, $n=\prod_{p}p^{\beta_p}$ be the prime factorizations (**Basic Knowledge: Prime Factorization and Highest Powers**). For each prime $p$: if $\alpha_p\ge\beta_p$, then by **Basic Knowledge: Order of a Power of an Element**, $a^{m/p^{\alpha_p}}$ has order $\frac{m}{\left(m,m/p^{\alpha_p}\right)}=\frac{m}{m/p^{\alpha_p}}=p^{\alpha_p}$; if $\beta_p>\alpha_p$, then $b^{n/p^{\beta_p}}$ has order $\frac{n}{\left(n,n/p^{\beta_p}\right)}=p^{\beta_p}$. Thus for each $p$ we obtain an element $x_p\in H$ of order $p^{\max\left(\alpha_p,\beta_p\right)}$, and for distinct primes $p$ these orders are pairwise coprime. Since $H$ is commutative, these $x_p$ commute pairwise, and applying **Basic Knowledge: Order of a Product of Commuting Elements of Coprime Order** repeatedly, the product $\prod_p x_p$ has order $\prod_p p^{\max\left(\alpha_p,\beta_p\right)}=\left[m,n\right]$. Hence $G$ necessarily contains an element of order $\left[m,n\right]$. $\blacksquare$

## 1.2-13

Let $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$ be the set of all $n\times n$ matrices whose entries are integers and whose determinant is $1$.

(1) Prove that $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$ is a subgroup of $GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$;

(2) Let $S=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$, $T=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$, $U=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$. Prove that any two of them generate $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

### Solution 1.2-13

**(1) Prove that $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)<GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$.** The identity matrix $I_n$ has integer entries and $\left|I_n\right|=1$, so $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$ is nonempty. For any $A,B\in SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$, $B$ has integer entries and $\left|B\right|=1$, so by **Basic Knowledge: Adjugate Matrices and Determinants of Integer Matrices**, all entries of $B^{-1}$ are integers, hence all entries of $AB^{-1}$ are integers. Moreover, by **Basic Knowledge: Adjugate Matrices and Determinants of Integer Matrices**, $\left|AB^{-1}\right|=\frac{\left|A\right|}{\left|B\right|}=\frac{1}{1}=1$. Hence $AB^{-1}\in SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)$. By **Theorem 1.2.2 (Subgroup Criterion)** (condition (3)), $SL\left(n,\mathbb Z\right)<GL\left(n,\mathbb R\right)$.

**(2) Prove that any two of $S,T,R,U$ generate $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.** First verify $S,T,R,U\in SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$: all entries of each matrix are integers, and direct computation gives $\left|S\right|=\left|T\right|=\left|R\right|=\left|U\right|=1$.

**First prove $\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.** For any $A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\in SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$, by **Basic Knowledge: Elementary Row Operations and the Euclidean Algorithm**, repeatedly multiplying on the left by $T^{k}$ and $U^{k}$ (i.e. the two kinds of elementary row operations) together with the Euclidean algorithm reduces $A$ to a diagonal matrix; since $\left|A\right|=1$ and elementary row operations do not change the absolute value of the determinant, the diagonal entries of the resulting diagonal matrix are $\pm1$ with product $\pm1$. Since $S^{2}=-I$ expresses $-I$, $A$ can be reduced to the identity $I$ using $S,T$. Conversely, $S,T\in SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$, so $\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

We now prove that the subgroup generated by each of the remaining pairs equals $\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$.

**$\left\langle S,R\right\rangle$ and $\left\langle T,R\right\rangle$.** Direct computation gives

$$
ST=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}=R.
$$

Hence $R=ST$, so $R\in\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$, and $T=S^{-1}R\in\left\langle S,R\right\rangle$, $S=RT^{-1}\in\left\langle T,R\right\rangle$. Thus $\left\langle S,R\right\rangle\supseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$ and $\left\langle T,R\right\rangle\supseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$; since $S,T$ appear in the expressions in terms of $S,R$ (or $T,R$), the reverse inclusions are obvious, so $\left\langle S,R\right\rangle=\left\langle T,R\right\rangle=\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

**$\left\langle S,U\right\rangle$.** Compute $S^{-1}TS$:

$$
S^{-1}TS=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}=U^{-1}.
$$

Hence $T=SU^{-1}S^{-1}\in\left\langle S,U\right\rangle$, so $\left\langle S,U\right\rangle\supseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$. Also $U=S^{-1}T^{-1}S\in\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$, so $\left\langle S,U\right\rangle\subseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$. Therefore $\left\langle S,U\right\rangle=\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

**$\left\langle T,U\right\rangle$.** $T=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $U=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ are precisely the two kinds of elementary transformation matrices in **Basic Knowledge: Elementary Row Operations and the Euclidean Algorithm**; by the Euclidean algorithm, using only these two kinds of elementary row operations reduces any element of $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$ to $\pm I$, so $\left\langle T,U\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

**$\left\langle R,U\right\rangle$.** From $R=ST$ we get $T=S^{-1}R$, and $U^{-1}=S^{-1}TS$; substituting gives

$$
U^{-1}=S^{-1}\left(S^{-1}R\right)S=S^{-2}RS.
$$

Since $S^{2}=-I$, we have $S^{-2}=\left(-I\right)^{-1}=-I$, hence $U^{-1}=-IRS=-RS$, i.e. $S=-R^{-1}U^{-1}$. Direct computation gives $R^{3}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}=-I$, so $-I=R^{3}\in\left\langle R,U\right\rangle$, and hence

$$
S=\left(-I\right)\left(-S\right)=\left(-I\right)\left(R^{-1}U^{-1}\right)=R^{3}R^{-1}U^{-1}=R^{2}U^{-1}\in\left\langle R,U\right\rangle.
$$

Then $T=S^{-1}R\in\left\langle R,U\right\rangle$, so $\left\langle R,U\right\rangle\supseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$. Since $R,U\in SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$, the reverse inclusion $\left\langle R,U\right\rangle\subseteq\left\langle S,T\right\rangle$ is obvious. Hence $\left\langle R,U\right\rangle=\left\langle S,T\right\rangle=SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$.

In summary, any two of $S,T,R,U$ generate $SL\left(2,\mathbb Z\right)$. $\blacksquare$

## 1.2-20

Let $H_{1},H_{2}$ be two subgroups of a finite group $G$. Prove that

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]\le\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right].
$$

Moreover, if $\left[G:H_{1}\right]$ and $\left[G:H_{2}\right]$ are coprime, then

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]
$$

and $G=H_{1}H_{2}$.

### Solution 1.2-20

**First prove the inequality.** Consider the map

$$
\varphi:G/\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)\to G/H_{1}\times G/H_{2},\quad g\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)\mapsto\left(gH_{1},gH_{2}\right).
$$

**Well-defined**: if $g\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)=g'\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)$, then by **Lemma 1.2.12 (Coset Coincidence Criterion)**, $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}$, so $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}$ and $g^{-1}g'\in H_{2}$, and by **Lemma 1.2.12 (Coset Coincidence Criterion)** again, $gH_{1}=g'H_{1}$ and $gH_{2}=g'H_{2}$; hence $\varphi$ is well-defined.

**Injectivity**: if $\left(gH_{1},gH_{2}\right)=\left(g'H_{1},g'H_{2}\right)$, then by **Lemma 1.2.12 (Coset Coincidence Criterion)**, $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}$ and $g^{-1}g'\in H_{2}$, so $g^{-1}g'\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}$, and by **Lemma 1.2.12 (Coset Coincidence Criterion)** again, $g\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)=g'\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)$. Hence $\varphi$ is injective.

Therefore $\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left|G/\left(H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right)\right|\le\left|G/H_{1}\right|\cdot\left|G/H_{2}\right|=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]$.

**Next prove equality in the coprime case.** By **Corollary 1.2.23 (Multiplicativity of the Index)**, since $H_{1}\cap H_{2}<H_{1}<G$,

$$
\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[H_{1}:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right].
$$

Hence $\left[G:H_{1}\right]\mid\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]$. Similarly, from $H_{1}\cap H_{2}<H_{2}<G$, we get $\left[G:H_{2}\right]\mid\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]$. Since $\left[G:H_{1}\right]$ and $\left[G:H_{2}\right]$ are coprime, by **Basic Knowledge: Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple**, $\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]\mid\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]$. Combined with the inequality $\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]\le\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]$ just proved, we get $\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]=\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]$.

**Finally prove $G=H_{1}H_{2}$.** By **Basic Knowledge: Order of a Product of Finite Subsets**,

$$
\left|H_{1}H_{2}\right|=\frac{\left|H_{1}\right|\left|H_{2}\right|}{\left|H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right|}.
$$

By **Theorem 1.2.22 (Lagrange's Theorem)**, $\left|H_{1}\right|=\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{1}\right]}$, $\left|H_{2}\right|=\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{2}\right]}$, and

$$
\left|H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right|=\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{1}\cap H_{2}\right]}=\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]}.
$$

Substituting,

$$
\left|H_{1}H_{2}\right|=\frac{\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{1}\right]}\cdot\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{2}\right]}}{\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left[G:H_{1}\right]\left[G:H_{2}\right]}}=\left|G\right|.
$$

Since $H_{1}H_{2}\subseteq G$ and the two have the same number of elements, $G=H_{1}H_{2}$. $\blacksquare$
